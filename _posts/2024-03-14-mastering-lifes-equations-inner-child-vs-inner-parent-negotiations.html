There is never good or bad in life. The world lives in equilibrium and the satisfaction we get in life is through making tradeoffs that we are comfortable with. Keeping a strict diet to ensure you attain the body you dream of? Then you cannot eat anything that you want. Accepting a job opportunity that promises big rewards through hard work? Then you have to say no to everything else. Do you ever feel restless? Overwhelmed? Feeling that you should do one thing but the moment you take a step, suddenly an insurmountable wave of dread and tiredness overcomes you. The key to solving this problem, is by harnessing your inner child and inner parent — facilitating the negotiations between them.

In the video about self-sabotage1 by Heidi Priebe, she explains that your inner child is what you require in the short term, your subconscious needs and wants, while your inner parent controls the wheel with the consciousness: your goals, morals, ethics, ego. Perhaps you are confronted with a work offer that pushes you out of your comfort zone — interacting with strangers, working long hours, spending a significant amount of time in a foreign environment. Your inner parent says hell yes to this chance, but your days feel… off. You’re worried and anxious, why? This is what you need and want right? You just need to push yourself. Meanwhile, your inner child is going off the rails — all it ever wants is security and protection. “Why am I here when it’s not what I really want to do?”

Conflict and restlessness arises. The piece to the puzzle is bringing awareness to the benefits and disadvantages of the decisions you make, negotiating these demands between your inner child and parent. “Perhaps I won’t keep as strict of a diet, I just want to be fit”, “I know I’m sacrificing time with my friends, but I’m making the decision to value my career more.”

When they both live in harmony and it is transparent what the respective compromises and demands are, restlessness eases. Of course, that’s not to say it won’t be hard — the things you need to do the most are often the most difficult.

Personal Takeaways:

In recent years, I’ve felt a deep sense of restlessness and unease. Learning mathematics, economics, statistics and finance introduced me to the realm of quantitative finance — a world where entry-level jobs offer upwards of half a million annual salaries. Multiple opportunities presented themselves: case competitions, networking events, quant projects. At every single step, I would encounter immense internal resistance. I just wasn’t interested, and there was zero meaning that I could obtain from them. I had a lot of moments where I felt inspired and motivated, but acting on them made me so unfulfilled and I derived no satisfaction despite the effort I put in.

I saw a video and image2 by Heidi Priebe again which discussed growing pain versus shrinking pain (clearly I went down a rabbit-hole). Growing pain is the discomfort you feel when you learn something new, you feel the nervousness and the stress, but your gut instinct tells you that you are moving in the right direction and you are attuned to your values. Shrinking pain is when it feels like you are betraying yourself, discomfort due to putting limitations on yourself in an effort to “grow”. Learning about mathematics, statistics, machine learning all feels uncomfortable. Sometimes it is really demotivating and even makes me want to give up, but my gut instinct pushes me forward because it is the right move. Learning about quantitative finance feels the same way, but my gut instinct cannot push me forward because it knows that I’m doing it for external reasons outside of myself.

Forcing myself to speak in foreign environments and interact with strangers is an intensely difficult, stressful and painful endeavour, but my gut instinct knows I am moving towards the right direction. Forcing myself to filter what I say, act “nice” and induce people-pleasing behaviour feels deeply concerning — I am betraying myself by not letting my personality shine in an attempt to gain external approval. Honest conflict leads to more rewarding and fulfilling interactions and relationships.

Understanding the difference between my inner child and inner parent, in addition to how it connects to a sense of growing pain versus a shrinking pain, has improved the harmony within my own soul. I do not completely understand the difference in every situation which creates growing pain, but I know within myself that I am on the right track.
